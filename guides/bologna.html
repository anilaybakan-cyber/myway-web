Bologna

La Rossa, La Dotta, La Grassa — the Red, the Learned, the Fat

✍️ MyWay Editorial Team • 🛡️ Verified Local Guide
Overview & Atmosphere

The True Vibe of Bologna

Bologna has three nicknames and every one earns its place. La Rossa — the red — for the terracotta rooftops and medieval brickwork that glow at golden hour. La Dotta — the learned — for the world's oldest university, founded in 1088, still filling every piazza with students and ideas. La Grassa — the fat — because Bologna invented tagliatelle al ragù, mortadella, tortellini, and tortelloni, and the Bolognese are entirely unapologetic about it. This is Italy's best-kept secret: a real city, with real food, real history, and almost no tourist crowds.

🗣️ Local Concept: Sfogline (sfol-YEE-neh)

The women who hand-roll pasta in Bologna — a centuries-old tradition. When a Bolognese says the sfoglina made the pasta, it means it's the real thing. The highest compliment a dish can receive. Look for it on menus and trust it completely.

3 Unwritten Rules for Bologna

1

Bologna is a walking city — wear good shoes

The historic center is compact, flat, and almost entirely walkable. The 40 km of UNESCO-listed porticoes keep you dry in rain and cool in sun. You will not need taxis or buses once you're in the center. Just walk — that's the entire point.

2

Eat lunch — not dinner — as your main meal

Bolognese culinary tradition centers on the midday meal. Restaurants are at their best, freshest, and most local at lunchtime. Many trattorias serve a Menu del Giorno — two or three courses, wine included, at remarkable value. This is how you eat here.

3

Bolognese ragù is NOT what you think

The real thing is slow-cooked, meat-heavy, with very little tomato — almost orange, not red. It is served with tagliatelle, never spaghetti. If a restaurant serves 'spaghetti bolognese' it is not a real Bolognese restaurant. Walk past it without looking back.

Getting Around

Bologna Public Transit & Airport Transfers

Bologna is Italy's most walkable city. Once you're in the historic center, you need almost no transport at all. Getting there from the airport is where the choices matter.

The Marconi Express monorail is the fastest — 7 minutes from the airport directly to Bologna Centrale station, departures every 15 minutes. From Centrale, the historic center is a 15-minute walk. The Aerobus shuttle is cheaper and drops you near Piazza Maggiore — good if your hotel is in the center. Taxis take about 20 minutes and are significantly more expensive.

The entire historic center is about 2 km across. The 40 km of UNESCO-listed porticoes mean you can walk in any weather completely sheltered. From Bologna Centrale to Piazza Maggiore is a 15-minute walk. Almost everywhere you need to go is within 20 minutes on foot.

You'll rarely need a bus in the center, but they're useful for reaching San Luca basilica, Giardini Margherita park, or neighborhoods outside the walls. Buy tickets at tabacchi shops, machines at stops, or the Roger/Muver apps. Validate on board.

Bologna is very cycle-friendly with dedicated lanes throughout the center. Rent bikes via BikeMi or Mobike apps, or from rental shops near the station. The flat terrain makes it easy and fast. Perfect for reaching neighborhoods like Bolognina or the university quarter.

Bologna Centrale is one of Italy's great rail hubs. Florence is 35 minutes by high-speed train, Milan 1 hour, Venice 1.5 hours, Rome 2 hours. Modena — home to the world's greatest balsamic vinegar and Ferrari museum — is just 17 minutes. One of the best bases in all of Italy.

⚡ Pro Tip

To reach the Sanctuary of San Luca, walk the world's longest porticoed arcade — 3.8 km, 666 arches from Porta Saragozza. It takes 60–90 minutes uphill but the views and the achievement are worth every step. Or take the San Luca Express tourist train if you prefer.

Off The Beaten Path

Handpicked Hidden Gems in Bologna

Bologna rewards the curious like few cities in Italy. The best things here are behind unmarked doors, up medieval towers, and through tiny windows.

Finestrella di Via Piella

A tiny window cut into a wall on Via Piella — look through it and see one of Bologna's secret canals, reflecting the medieval buildings above. Called La Piccola Venezia (Little Venice). Go early morning or late evening for the best light. Walk 20 meters further for a second canal view through a grate in the street.

Osteria del Sole — no sign on the door

Open since 1465, hidden in the Quadrilatero with no sign outside. Inside: low ceilings, long shared wooden tables, locals drinking wine at noon. No food served — bring your own from the market stalls outside. Order a glass of house wine at the counter. One of the most genuine places in Italy.

Santo Stefano — the Seven Churches

From the outside: one bulky medieval building. Inside: a complex of seven interconnected churches from different centuries. Dating back to the 5th century. One of Bologna's most extraordinary and most overlooked monuments. Almost no queue, almost no tourists. Free entry.

Archiginnasio Anatomical Theatre

Inside the Archiginnasio — once the main building of the world's oldest university — a fully intact 17th-century anatomical theatre carved entirely from wood, where early medical students watched dissections by candlelight. One of the most extraordinary rooms in Italy. Small entry fee. Almost always crowd-free.

Biblioteca Salaborsa — glass floor to ancient Rome

The public library next to Piazza Maggiore has a transparent glass floor — look down through it at the excavated ruins of Roman roads and buildings. Free to enter. Almost no tourist knows it's there. Go, look down, say nothing.

The Fountain of Neptune — one specific angle

From a very specific angle — directly from the southwest corner — the thumb of Neptune's outstretched hand creates a suggestive optical illusion that sculptor Giambologna deliberately hid from the censors in 1566. Find the angle. You'll know it immediately.
