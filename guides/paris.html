Paris

The city everyone thinks they know — and almost no one does

✍️ MyWay Editorial Team • 🛡️ Verified Local Guide
Overview & Atmosphere

The True Vibe of Paris

Paris is the most visited city on earth, which means it has spent decades learning to perform for tourists. The Paris most visitors see — the selfie spots, the overpriced cafés near the monuments, the Eiffel Tower queues — is a city built for consumption, not for living. The real Paris runs parallel to it: in the neighbourhood bakeries, the covered passages from 1820, the parks where locals play pétanque (a French bowling game) on Wednesday afternoons, the wine bars with no sign and no English menu. This city rewards the curious and ignores the passive. The monuments are genuinely extraordinary — the Louvre, Notre-Dame, Versailles — but they are the beginning of Paris, not the end of it. The question is not whether to see the Eiffel Tower. The question is what you do after.

🗣️ Local Concept: Arrondissement (ah-ron-dees-MOHN)

Paris is divided into 20 arrondissements — numbered 1 to 20 in a clockwise spiral from the city centre. Each has its own character, its own markets, its own pace. The 1st is the historic core; the 11th is where Parisians go out; the 18th is Montmartre; the 20th is Belleville. Knowing which arrondissement you're heading to tells you more about your evening than any restaurant review.

3 Unwritten Rules for Paris

1

Say bonjour — every single time

In Paris, entering a shop, café, or bakery without saying bonjour is considered rude. Not aggressive, not tourist-bashing — just genuinely impolite. Say bonjour when you enter, merci and au revoir when you leave. This single habit will change how every interaction feels. Parisians are not cold; they are formal with strangers. The formality is the door.

2

The métro is the city's bloodstream

Paris has 16 metro lines, 302 stations, and covers almost every corner of the city. The Navigo Easy card — Paris's equivalent of London's Oyster Card — is the most practical option for short stays: buy it at any station, load t+ tickets onto it, and tap the reader at every metro, RER, bus, or tram boarding. Lines 1 and 14 run driverless and operate 24 hours at weekends. The metro runs until around 1am on weekdays and 2am on weekends.

3

Lunch is the meal — not dinner

The best-value eating in Paris is the weekday lunch. Most bistros and brasseries offer a formule — a set menu of two or three courses at a fraction of the evening price. The same kitchen, the same food, half the cost. Locals eat lunch between 12:30 and 14:00. Arrive before 12:30 or after 13:30 to avoid waiting. Dinner reservations at good restaurants are essential, often weeks ahead.

Getting Around

Paris Public Transit & Airport Transfers

Paris is built for walking and the metro handles everything else. The city is more compact than it appears — many famous landmarks are closer together than visitors expect.

RER B train connects CDG directly to the city centre in about 35 minutes, stopping at Gare du Nord, Châtelet-Les Halles, Saint-Michel, and Luxembourg. Runs frequently and is by far the most reliable option. Taxis are expensive and traffic-dependent. The Roissybus coach is slower but drops at Opéra. Always take the RER B.

16 lines cover the entire city. The Navigo Easy card is Paris's equivalent of London's Oyster Card — buy it at any station, load t+ tickets onto it, and tap the reader at metro and RER gates or on boarding buses and trams. Lines 1 and 14 are driverless and run 24 hours at weekends. On busy lines, move toward the doors before your stop; they close fast.

Paris buses use the same t+ ticket as the metro and offer something the metro cannot: the city above ground. Line 69 goes from the Eiffel Tower to Père Lachaise through the heart of the city. Line 29 links Opéra, the Marais, and Bastille. Any bus along the Seine is a free tour of central Paris. Worth taking at least once — especially at night, when the monuments are lit.

Paris's public bike-share scheme has thousands of docking stations across the city. A short-term subscription covers 30 or 45 minutes per trip (classic or electric). The Seine riverside path, Canal Saint-Martin, and the Bois de Boulogne are all well-served by bike. Central Paris is largely flat. The best urban cycling in France.

Paris rewards walking above almost any other mode of transport. The distance from the Louvre to Notre-Dame is 20 minutes on foot. Montmartre to the Opéra is 30 minutes. The Seine takes you from the Eiffel Tower to the Île de la Cité in 45 minutes. Walking connects the things between the things — the passages, the courtyards, the bakeries you'd miss underground.

⚡ Pro Tip

Versailles is 40 minutes from central Paris on the RER C — no car, no tour bus needed. Take the train to Versailles-Château-Rive Gauche, walk 10 minutes to the palace. Go on a Tuesday or Wednesday when French school groups are less likely to be there. Arrive at 9am when the gates open.

Off The Beaten Path

Handpicked Hidden Gems in Paris

Paris hides its best things inside other things — chapels inside palaces, parks inside railways, gardens inside ministries. The most interesting Paris is always one door further in.

Sainte-Chapelle — Île de la Cité

Ten minutes from Notre-Dame, hidden behind the walls of the Palais de Justice, Sainte-Chapelle is arguably the most extraordinary interior in Paris. Built in six years between 1242 and 1248 by Louis IX to house the Crown of Thorns, its upper chapel is almost entirely made of stained glass — 1,113 biblical scenes across 600 square metres. On a sunny day the light is something close to supernatural. Two-thirds of the glass is original 13th-century work. Ticketed entry; book in advance online. Go on a sunny morning when the light is on the glass.

Coulée Verte René-Dumont — 12th arrondissement

Built on top of a disused 19th-century railway viaduct, the Coulée Verte is an elevated garden walkway stretching 4.5km through the 12th arrondissement. It opened in 1993 — seventeen years before New York's High Line, which was directly inspired by it. Below the viaduct arches: galleries, design studios, and workshops. Above: wisteria, roses, bamboo, and a Paris no tourist visits. Free entry. Start at the Bastille end and walk east.

Arènes de Lutèce — 5th arrondissement

In the Latin Quarter, tucked behind an ordinary apartment building on Rue Monge, a Gallo-Roman amphitheatre from the 1st century AD sits largely forgotten. It once held 15,000 spectators. Today it holds locals playing pétanque (a French bowling game) on weekday afternoons, children on the grass, and pigeons. No entrance fee, no ticket desk, no tour groups. One of the oldest surviving structures in Paris. Almost no one visits.

Galerie Vivienne — 2nd arrondissement

Paris has 20 surviving covered passages — glass-roofed shopping arcades from the 1820s and 1830s, predecessors of the modern shopping mall. Galerie Vivienne is the most beautiful: mosaic floors, Neoclassical reliefs, a wine merchant who has been there for decades, a tearoom, a bookshop specialising in old and rare editions. Free to enter. Walk slowly. This is what Paris looked like before Haussmann tore it apart.

Palais Royal Gardens — 1st arrondissement

Steps from the Louvre, the Palais Royal gardens are enclosed by colonnaded arcades on three sides — a quiet rectangle of gravel paths, fountains, and lime trees, surrounded by antique dealers, a restaurant, and the Daniel Buren striped columns in the courtyard. Despite being in the most visited square kilometre in the world, it remains largely peaceful. Cardinals Richelieu and Mazarin lived here. Victor Hugo used to walk here. Free entry. On a warm afternoon, so should you.

La Petite Ceinture — 15th/16th arrondissement

The Petite Ceinture was a circular railway that encircled Paris from 1862 to 1934. Abandoned for nearly a century, it has been partially reclaimed by wildflowers, birch trees, foxes, and the occasional urban farmer. Several sections are accessible to the public — the 15th and 16th arrondissement sections are the most interesting. Free, open to the public. Walking the overgrown tracks is one of the strangest and most beautiful things Paris offers.

(sahn-TAY)
