Paris

The city everyone thinks they know — and almost no one does

✍️ MyWay Editorial Team • 🛡️ Verified Local Guide
Overview & Atmosphere

The True Vibe of Paris

Paris is the most visited city on earth, which means it has spent decades learning to perform for tourists. The Paris most visitors see — the selfie spots, the overpriced cafés near the monuments, the Eiffel Tower queues — is a city built for consumption, not for living. The real Paris runs parallel to it: in the neighbourhood bakeries, the covered passages from 1820, the parks where locals play pétanque (a French bowling game) on Wednesday afternoons, the wine bars with no sign and no English menu. This city rewards the curious and ignores the passive. The monuments are genuinely extraordinary — the Louvre, Notre-Dame, Versailles — but they are the beginning of Paris, not the end of it. The question is not whether to see the Eiffel Tower. The question is what you do after.

🗣️ Local Concept: Arrondissement (ah-ron-dees-MOHN)

Paris is divided into 20 arrondissements — numbered 1 to 20 in a clockwise spiral from the city centre. Each has its own character, its own markets, its own pace. The 1st is the historic core; the 11th is where Parisians go out; the 18th is Montmartre; the 20th is Belleville. Knowing which arrondissement you're heading to tells you more about your evening than any restaurant review.

3 Unwritten Rules for Paris

1

Say bonjour — every single time

In Paris, entering a shop, café, or bakery without saying bonjour is considered rude. Not aggressive, not tourist-bashing — just genuinely impolite. Say bonjour when you enter, merci and au revoir when you leave. This single habit will change how every interaction feels. Parisians are not cold; they are formal with strangers. The formality is the door.

2

The métro is the city's bloodstream

Paris has 16 metro lines, 302 stations, and covers almost every corner of the city. The Navigo Easy card — Paris's equivalent of London's Oyster Card — is the most practical option for short stays: buy it at any station, load t+ tickets onto it, and tap the reader at every metro, RER, bus, or tram boarding. Lines 1 and 14 run driverless and operate 24 hours at weekends. The metro runs until around 1am on weekdays and 2am on weekends.

3

Lunch is the meal — not dinner

The best-value eating in Paris is the weekday lunch. Most bistros and brasseries offer a formule — a set menu of two or three courses at a fraction of the evening price. The same kitchen, the same food, half the cost. Locals eat lunch between 12:30 and 14:00. Arrive before 12:30 or after 13:30 to avoid waiting. Dinner reservations at good restaurants are essential, often weeks ahead.

Getting Around

Paris Public Transit & Airport Transfers

Paris is built for walking and the metro handles everything else. The city is more compact than it appears — many famous landmarks are closer together than visitors expect.

✈️

CDG Airport → City Centre

RER B train connects CDG directly to the city centre in about 35 minutes, stopping at Gare du Nord, Châtelet-Les Halles, Saint-Michel, and Luxembourg. Runs frequently and is by far the most reliable option. Taxis are expensive and traffic-dependent. The Roissybus coach is slower but drops at Opéra. Always take the RER B.

🚇

Métro — the default

16 lines cover the entire city. The Navigo Easy card is Paris's equivalent of London's Oyster Card — buy it at any station, load t+ tickets onto it, and tap the reader at metro and RER gates or on boarding buses and trams. Lines 1 and 14 are driverless and run 24 hours at weekends. On busy lines, move toward the doors before your stop; they close fast.

🚌

Buses — the scenic option

Paris buses use the same t+ ticket as the metro and offer something the metro cannot: the city above ground. Line 69 goes from the Eiffel Tower to Père Lachaise through the heart of the city. Line 29 links Opéra, the Marais, and Bastille. Any bus along the Seine is a free tour of central Paris. Worth taking at least once — especially at night, when the monuments are lit.

🚲

Vélib' — city bikes

Paris's public bike-share scheme has thousands of docking stations across the city. A short-term subscription covers 30 or 45 minutes per trip (classic or electric). The Seine riverside path, Canal Saint-Martin, and the Bois de Boulogne are all well-served by bike. Central Paris is largely flat. The best urban cycling in France.

🚶

On foot — the best way

Paris rewards walking above almost any other mode of transport. The distance from the Louvre to Notre-Dame is 20 minutes on foot. Montmartre to the Opéra is 30 minutes. The Seine takes you from the Eiffel Tower to the Île de la Cité in 45 minutes. Walking connects the things between the things — the passages, the courtyards, the bakeries you'd miss underground.

💡 Versailles by RER

Versailles is 40 minutes from central Paris on the RER C — no car, no tour bus needed. Take the train to Versailles-Château-Rive Gauche, walk 10 minutes to the palace. Go on a Tuesday or Wednesday when French school groups are less likely to be there. Arrive at 9am when the gates open.

Neighborhood Guide

Where to Stay in Paris: Best Areas

Paris's 20 arrondissements are dramatically different from each other. Staying in the right one shapes the entire trip.

Le Marais (3rd & 4th arr.)

Most vibrant

The Marais is Paris at its most energetic and alive — medieval streets, the Place des Vosges, the Jewish quarter, an openly LGBTQ+ neighbourhood, the best falafel in the city, and galleries everywhere. Central, walkable, and genuinely busy at all hours. Expensive but worth it for a first visit.

📍 Most central • Best food streets • Always lively

Saint-Germain-des-Prés (6th arr.)

Most elegant

The Left Bank's most famous neighbourhood — Café de Flore, Les Deux Magots, Luxembourg Gardens around the corner. Expensive, beautiful, and more calm than the Marais. Where Sartre and Beauvoir wrote; where Parisians who can afford it live. Excellent base for the Musée d'Orsay and the Latin Quarter.

📍 Left Bank atmosphere • Luxembourg Gardens • Literary history

Bastille & Oberkampf (11th arr.)

Most local

Where Parisians actually go out. The 11th has the best natural wine bars, the best bistros, the best late-night energy in the city. Not a tourist neighbourhood — it's a living one. Slightly further from the monuments but well-connected by metro. The best arrondissement in Paris for eating and drinking.

📍 Best restaurants • Best bars • Where locals go out

Montmartre (18th arr.)

Most atmospheric

The village on the hill — cobbled streets, a vineyard, Sacré-Coeur, artists' studios, and views over the whole city. Touristy around the Sacré-Coeur itself, but a few streets away it becomes genuinely local. The best breakfast neighbourhood in Paris. Worth the hill.

📍 Village feel • Best views • Cobbled streets

Canal Saint-Martin (10th arr.)

Most creative

The canal neighbourhood — iron footbridges, plane trees, independent boutiques, the best coffee in Paris, natural wine bars, and a genuinely young local crowd. Not a tourist area at all. The neighbourhood Parisians under 35 talk about most. Excellent metro connections.

📍 Young and creative • Best coffee • Canal walks
Seasons & Weather

Best Time to Visit Paris

Paris works in every season, but each is a different city. The question is which version you want.

Spring

Mar – May
The Classic Season

April and May are when Paris earns its reputation. Cherry blossoms in the Tuileries, chestnut trees along every boulevard, the Luxembourg Gardens filling up at midday, terrace culture returning after winter. The light is extraordinary — golden, long, and particular to this city. Crowds haven't peaked yet, the weather is mild, and the city is genuinely at its most beautiful. The best Paris is a spring Paris.

Summer

Jun – Aug
Busy but Brilliant

June is the best summer month — long evenings, Paris Plages not yet started, crowds not yet at their peak. July brings Bastille Day (14 July) with the military parade on the Champs-Élysées and fireworks over the Eiffel Tower — one of the great free spectacles in Europe. August is when Parisians leave and tourists arrive; the city empties of locals and fills with visitors. Some restaurants and shops close for August.

Autumn

Sep – Nov
The Local's Season

September is when Paris wakes up. The cultural season begins — theatre, opera, art openings, fashion week. The summer tourists have left, the locals have returned from August, and the city finds its rhythm again. October brings the most beautiful light of the year: amber, low, and entirely Parisian. Nuit Blanche (first Saturday of October) turns the entire city into an open-air art installation for one night — free, extraordinary, and very local.

Winter

Dec – Feb
Quiet and Underrated

December brings Christmas markets along the Champs-Élysées and at La Défense, skating rinks at the Hôtel de Ville, and the city lit from end to end. January and February are the quietest months — museums nearly empty, restaurants bookable without weeks of planning, prices at their lowest. The grey Paris light of winter has its own beauty. This is when the city belongs entirely to those who live in it.

Culinary Culture

Authentic Local Dining & Food Secrets in Paris

French cuisine is the most influential culinary tradition in the world, and Paris is its capital. But the Paris worth eating in is not the one with English menus outside and tourist photos on the walls. It's the neighbourhood bistro on a Tuesday, the boulangerie at 8am, the wine bar with three tables and a handwritten list.

🥐

Croissant — quality varies by bakery

A great croissant is laminated: layers of butter-enriched dough visible in the cross-section, shatteringly crisp on the outside, yielding within. A mediocre croissant is soft, uniform, and forgettable. Paris has both. The difference is the baker. Maison Landemaine, Du Pain et des Idées, and Sébastien Gaudard are among the best. Buy from a boulangerie with a queue — queues don't lie.

⭐ Before 10am · standing at the counter · with a café crème
🥩

Steak frites — the Parisian standard

The measure of a bistrot. A proper steak frites uses onglet (hanger steak) or entrecôte, served with hand-cut fries and a béarnaise or shallot sauce. Always ordered à point (medium) or saignant (rare) — bien cuit (well done) is considered a waste of good beef. Le Relais de l'Entrecôte in Saint-Germain serves nothing else and has a permanent queue. Worth every minute of the wait.

⭐ Saignant or à point · with béarnaise · with a carafe of Bordeaux
🧀

Fromage — the ritual

In a French restaurant, cheese comes before dessert, not instead of it. A cheese course is a platter of three to five cheeses served with bread — no crackers, no chutney. Ask the server what's best today. Comté, Époisses, Brie de Meaux, Roquefort, Camembert. The rule: let it come to room temperature. Cold cheese is an insult to the cheesemaker.

⭐ Before dessert · ask what's best · with the rest of the wine
🥂

Natural wine — Paris's obsession

Paris leads the world in natural wine — low-intervention, minimal sulphites, often orange or pét-nat. The 11th arrondissement alone has more good natural wine bars than most cities have wine bars total. Le Servan, Septime Cave, Clown Bar, Le Verre Volé on Canal Saint-Martin. Order a glass and ask what the vigneron is doing with it. This is the most interesting wine scene in Europe.

⭐ Le Verre Volé · Septime Cave · Le Clown Bar
☕

Coffee — the correct way

A café in Paris means an espresso. A café crème is espresso with steamed milk. A noisette is espresso with a dash of cream. Cappuccino is understood but slightly regarded with suspicion after 11am. Drink it at the bar — it's faster, cheaper, and more Parisian. The third-wave specialty coffee scene (Ten Belles, Café Lomi, Coutume) has transformed Paris in a decade. Both traditions coexist.

⭐ At the bar · café or café crème · before noon for anything with milk
🥖

The baguette — daily ritual

Parisians buy bread every day. A traditional baguette (baguette de tradition) has a crackling crust, open crumb, and slight tang from long fermentation. It goes stale by evening — this is correct. The best are from boulangeries that prove their dough slowly. Each arrondissement has its own; ask a neighbour. The city holds an annual competition for the best baguette in Paris — the winner supplies the Élysée Palace for a year.

⭐ Baguette de tradition · from a boulangerie with a queue · same day
🍷 Marché d'Aligre — the real market

The Marché d'Aligre in the 12th arrondissement is the most local market in Paris — a covered hall plus an outdoor flea market running simultaneously, Tuesday to Sunday mornings. Cheaper than any other Paris market, crowded with actual Parisians, no English signage. Go on a Saturday morning, buy cheese and charcuterie, take it to the nearby Promenade Plantée. The most Parisian Saturday possible.

Off The Beaten Path

Handpicked Hidden Gems in Paris

Paris hides its best things inside other things — chapels inside palaces, parks inside railways, gardens inside ministries. The most interesting Paris is always one door further in.

✨ The most beautiful interior in Paris — and worth every cent of the ticket

Sainte-Chapelle — Île de la Cité

Ten minutes from Notre-Dame, hidden behind the walls of the Palais de Justice, Sainte-Chapelle is arguably the most extraordinary interior in Paris. Built in six years between 1242 and 1248 by Louis IX to house the Crown of Thorns, its upper chapel is almost entirely made of stained glass — 1,113 biblical scenes across 600 square metres. On a sunny day the light is something close to supernatural. Two-thirds of the glass is original 13th-century work. Ticketed entry; book in advance online. Go on a sunny morning when the light is on the glass.

✨ The world's first elevated park — New York copied it

Coulée Verte René-Dumont — 12th arrondissement

Built on top of a disused 19th-century railway viaduct, the Coulée Verte is an elevated garden walkway stretching 4.5km through the 12th arrondissement. It opened in 1993 — seventeen years before New York's High Line, which was directly inspired by it. Below the viaduct arches: galleries, design studios, and workshops. Above: wisteria, roses, bamboo, and a Paris no tourist visits. Free entry. Start at the Bastille end and walk east.

✨ A Roman amphitheatre — in the middle of Paris

Arènes de Lutèce — 5th arrondissement

In the Latin Quarter, tucked behind an ordinary apartment building on Rue Monge, a Gallo-Roman amphitheatre from the 1st century AD sits largely forgotten. It once held 15,000 spectators. Today it holds locals playing pétanque (a French bowling game) on weekday afternoons, children on the grass, and pigeons. No entrance fee, no ticket desk, no tour groups. One of the oldest surviving structures in Paris. Almost no one visits.

✨ The most beautiful covered passage in Paris

Galerie Vivienne — 2nd arrondissement

Paris has 20 surviving covered passages — glass-roofed shopping arcades from the 1820s and 1830s, predecessors of the modern shopping mall. Galerie Vivienne is the most beautiful: mosaic floors, Neoclassical reliefs, a wine merchant who has been there for decades, a tearoom, a bookshop specialising in old and rare editions. Free to enter. Walk slowly. This is what Paris looked like before Haussmann tore it apart.

✨ A secret garden belonging to a ministry

Palais Royal Gardens — 1st arrondissement

Steps from the Louvre, the Palais Royal gardens are enclosed by colonnaded arcades on three sides — a quiet rectangle of gravel paths, fountains, and lime trees, surrounded by antique dealers, a restaurant, and the Daniel Buren striped columns in the courtyard. Despite being in the most visited square kilometre in the world, it remains largely peaceful. Cardinals Richelieu and Mazarin lived here. Victor Hugo used to walk here. Free entry. On a warm afternoon, so should you.

✨ An abandoned railway reclaimed by nature

La Petite Ceinture — 15th/16th arrondissement

The Petite Ceinture was a circular railway that encircled Paris from 1862 to 1934. Abandoned for nearly a century, it has been partially reclaimed by wildflowers, birch trees, foxes, and the occasional urban farmer. Several sections are accessible to the public — the 15th and 16th arrondissement sections are the most interesting. Free, open to the public. Walking the overgrown tracks is one of the strangest and most beautiful things Paris offers.

✨ Palais Garnier — free from outside, ticketed inside

The Palais Garnier (Opéra Garnier) is one of the most extravagant buildings in the world — the model for the Phantom of the Opera, with a grand staircase, a Marc Chagall ceiling in the auditorium, and a hidden lake underneath. Visiting the interior requires a ticket and advance booking. Standing on Place de l'Opéra to take in the extraordinary façade, however, costs nothing — and the building is extraordinary even from outside.

Scam Prevention

Tourist Traps to Avoid & Smarter Alternatives

Paris has mastered the tourist trap — but the escape routes are just as well known. Memorise these four.

💍 TRAP

The 'gold ring' and petition games

Crews 'finding' a gold ring at your feet, or collecting signatures then demanding money, are a classic of the big squares.

💡 What to Do Instead: Don't stop, smile, say 'non merci' and walk. Bag in front, zip closed — the Paris métro rule.
🥐 TRAP

Eating and drinking on the Champs-Élysées

The world's most famous avenue is the city's weakest spot for food: mediocre kitchens at tourist prices.

💡 What to Do Instead: Step one street off the avenue, or point yourself at the Marais or Canal Saint-Martin: same budget, real Paris.
🎨 TRAP

The 'instant portrait' push on Place du Tertre

On Montmartre's painters' square, portraits that start uninvited end in haggling; prices are never stated up front.

💡 What to Do Instead: Ask the price first, or just watch. Montmartre's real beauty is two streets away, on the quiet staircases.
🎟️ TRAP

Street sellers of 'skip-the-line' tickets

Most 'skip-the-line' tickets sold around the Eiffel and the Louvre are fake or wildly marked up.

💡 What to Do Instead: Buy only from official sites, dated and timed; if the Eiffel is full, watch from Trocadéro — if the Louvre is full, the Orsay is always calmer.
🛡️ The golden rule

One question tells you if a place is a tourist trap: does the waiter open in French? If yes, you're in the right place.

Local Slang

Useful Everyday Phrases in Paris

In Paris it isn't fluency that matters, it's courtesy; these five are enough.

🗣️ Bonjour
Hello — the password to everything; ask for nothing before saying it
🗣️ Merci
Thank you — on the way out too: merci, au revoir!
🗣️ S'il vous plaît
Please — un café, s'il vous plaît: your most-used sentence
🗣️ Santé!
Cheers! — eye contact while toasting is a French rule
🗣️ Au revoir
Goodbye — leaving a shop silently is rude; say this
