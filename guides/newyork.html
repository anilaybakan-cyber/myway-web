New York

The city that doesn't explain itself — and doesn't need to

✍️ MyWay Editorial Team • 🛡️ Verified Local Guide

Overview & Atmosphere

The True Vibe of New York

New York doesn't have a single centre — it has five boroughs, hundreds of neighbourhoods, and more than eight million people who are completely indifferent to your being there. That is the point. The subway runs 24 hours. The delis have been open since before your parents were born. New York bagels are made with the city's naturally soft water — bakers elsewhere can't replicate the texture. The pizza is folded in half and eaten while walking. There is always something happening three blocks away that no travel guide will tell you about. The city rewards the curious and punishes anyone who expects it to be convenient.

🗣️ Local Concept: Bodega (bo-DAY-ga)

A small neighbourhood convenience store — open at all hours, often with a deli counter, definitely with a cat somewhere inside. The bodega bacon, egg, and cheese on a roll is New York's breakfast. Not the restaurant version: the version made on a flat-top grill behind the counter, wrapped in foil, eaten on the kerb or the subway. It is one of the greatest things the city has produced.

3 Unwritten Rules for New York

1

The subway goes everywhere — use it

472 stations, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. Single ride $3.00, tap with any contactless card or phone — no MetroCard needed. Weekly fare cap $35: after 12 rides in a week, the rest are free. The subway is not always clean or quiet or on time. It is always faster than a taxi in Manhattan. Stand clear of the closing doors.

2

Manhattan is smaller than you think — Brooklyn is larger

Manhattan is 21 kilometres long and 4 kilometres wide at its widest. You can walk from the Lower East Side to Central Park in under an hour. Brooklyn, by contrast, contains entire worlds — Williamsburg, DUMBO, Park Slope, Crown Heights, Coney Island — each with its own character, none of them close to each other. Don't underestimate the distances in the outer boroughs.

3

Book restaurants; don't book views

The best views in New York are free or cheap: Brooklyn Bridge (walk across, free), Staten Island Ferry (free), Roosevelt Island Tram (subway fare), High Line (free). The Empire State Building and One World Observatory are ticketed and expensive — try the free options first. To visit the Statue of Liberty up close requires a separate ferry ticket; seeing it from the Staten Island Ferry is free and often enough. Times Square: overwhelming in the daytime, worth one evening for the lights — but eat somewhere else. The best restaurant tables — Via Carota, Don Angie, Lilia — book weeks ahead.

Getting Around

New York Public Transit & Airport Transfers

Take the metro or walk. Taxis only make sense late at night.

✈️ Airport → City

From JFK: AirTrain to Jamaica or Howard Beach, then subway — around 60 minutes, both ticketed separately. From LaGuardia: no rail link. Take the M60 bus to 125th Street subway station and continue to the city centre by metro. A direct taxi is also an option. From Newark: NJ Transit train to Penn Station, around 45 minutes. Taxis run on a meter; traffic makes both the time and the cost unpredictable.

🚇 Subway — the city's backbone

472 stations across five boroughs, 24 hours. Tap a contactless card, phone or OMNY Card on the turnstile reader. $3.25, weekly cap $35. Lines run in two modes: local trains stop at every station, express trains skip some — check the map before you board or you might overshoot. Trains run less frequently at night but no line closes.

🚌 Buses — slower but useful

Same fare as the metro, tap OMNY. Useful for crosstown routes the metro doesn't cover: M15, M34, M42. Slower than the metro in traffic, but you're watching the city go by — sometimes that's worth it.

⛴️ Staten Island Ferry — free, always running

Leaves from Whitehall Terminal every 30 minutes, 24 hours a day. 25 minutes past the Statue of Liberty, Governors Island and the lower Manhattan skyline — free in both directions. New Yorkers use it to commute; you'll be getting the best free boat ride in the world.

🚶 Walking — the only way to actually see it

Manhattan is a grid. Avenues run north-south; streets run east-west. Numbers increase heading north and west. Each block takes about one minute on foot — 20 blocks is roughly a mile. Below 14th Street the grid breaks down and you'll need the map. The outer boroughs are not grids. Wear shoes you can walk in for six hours.

💡 Roosevelt Island Tram — $3, no line, better than any rooftop bar

The Roosevelt Island Tram runs from 59th Street and Second Avenue in Manhattan across the East River to Roosevelt Island. Same fare as the subway — no extra charge. It climbs 76 metres above the river — views of the Queensboro Bridge, the East River, the Manhattan and Queens skylines. The ride takes five minutes each way. New Yorkers use it to commute. Everyone else ignores it in favour of $40 observation decks. Go at sunset.

Neighborhood Guide

Where to Stay in New York: Best Areas

Manhattan is convenient and expensive. Brooklyn is interesting and cheaper. The outer boroughs are where the city actually lives.

Lower East Side / East Village Most interesting

German, Jewish, Italian, Chinese, Puerto Rican — the neighbourhood where successive waves of immigrants arrived and left their mark. Katz's has been on the same corner since 1888. Russ & Daughters since 1914. The tenement buildings, the pickle shops, the narrow streets are still here. The East Village next door is younger, louder, cheaper. Both are lively well into the night.

Katz's & Russ & Daughters • Most historical • Bars until late
West Village / Greenwich Village Most charming

Where the grid breaks and the streets become names instead of numbers. Brownstones, narrow lanes, jazz clubs that have been running since the 1950s. The most expensive neighbourhood in Manhattan, square foot for square foot. Via Carota on Grove Street is the best Italian restaurant in the city. The White Horse Tavern has been serving since 1880. Good for walking; stays loud late into the night, so not ideal if you need early mornings. Friends fan? The building used for exterior shots is on Grove Street, a few steps from Via Carota.

Via Carota • Best streets for walking • Jazz history
Midtown / Hell's Kitchen Most convenient

Hell's Kitchen is Midtown's west side — walkable to Central Park, Times Square, Penn Station, and Lincoln Center. Less expensive than the Village, better connected than Brooklyn. The restaurant scene is genuinely good and underappreciated. The downside: it is Midtown. The noise, the pace, and the lack of neighbourhood feel are real. Best if transit convenience is the priority.

Central Park in 10 min • Penn Station nearby • Underrated food scene
Williamsburg / Brooklyn Most local

The most visited neighbourhood in Brooklyn — no longer the secret it once was, but still genuinely good. The L train runs to Manhattan in 10 minutes. The East River waterfront has the best skyline view in the city. The food and bar scene is excellent. Bedford Avenue is too crowded on weekends; go on a Tuesday evening — one of the most interesting stretches of street in the city at that hour.

Best Manhattan skyline views • L train to Manhattan • Best bars in Brooklyn
Upper West Side Most liveable

The neighbourhood New Yorkers move to when they want to stay in Manhattan. Central Park to the east, Riverside Park to the west. Barney Greengrass on Amsterdam Avenue has been serving sturgeon and smoked fish since 1908. Go in the morning. Quieter than downtown, close to everything by metro.

Central Park access • Barney Greengrass • Quiet and residential

Seasons & Weather

Best Time to Visit New York

New York is a different city depending on which season you visit. Summer is brutal, winter is harsh, autumn is breathtaking, spring is unpredictable.

Autumn Sep – Nov
The Best Season

September and October are when New York is most itself. The summer tourists have gone, the heat has broken, the light turns amber and low. In October, Central Park's autumn foliage looks exactly like it does in the films — amber light filtering between the skyscrapers, the park turning gold and red. The restaurant scene comes back after summer — chefs return, new openings happen in the autumn. Halloween in New York is a full cultural event. The New York Marathon in early November closes the city's streets in the best possible way. If you can only go once, go in October.

Spring Mar – May
Tentative and Worth It

March is still winter. April is unpredictable — one day a coat, the next a t-shirt. When May arrives the city opens up: trees in bloom, pavements filling, parks coming back to life. Cherry blossoms at the Brooklyn Botanic Garden peak in late April — it gets crowded and it's worth it. Governors Island opens its summer programme in May.

Summer Jun – Aug
Hot, Humid, Crowded

July in Manhattan has its own smell: hot asphalt, humidity, food from every direction. Metro platforms are like ovens. But the city doesn't stop: free outdoor concerts in Central Park (SummerStage), Shakespeare in the Park, and the Rockaway beaches are 60 minutes away on the A train. In summer, early morning and late evening are the best hours to be outside.

Winter Dec – Feb
Cold, Cheap, Genuinely Atmospheric

December is the most expensive month — the Rockefeller Center tree, the lights, the crowds, the prices. January and February are when the city empties of tourists; hotels drop by half, museums are quiet, restaurant tables are easy to find. When it snows everything changes: the streets go silent, Manhattan turns into a film set, Central Park becomes the most beautiful place in the world for a few hours. Get out before the ploughs arrive.

Culinary Culture

Authentic Local Dining & Food Secrets in New York

New York has every cuisine on earth and opinions about all of them. Start with what the city invented.

🥩 Pastrami on rye — Katz's Delicatessen

Katz's has been at 205 East Houston Street since 1888. You take a ticket at the door, walk to the counter, and ask the man to slice the pastrami for you. He will. The sandwich arrives piled obscenely high on rye bread with mustard. You eat it at a Formica table under fluorescent lights and signed celebrity photographs. The pastrami is hand-carved, fatty, and slow-cured. This is not a restaurant recommendation — it is an obligation.

⭐ 205 E Houston St · take a ticket · weekday mornings less crowded · ticketed
🥯 Bagel with lox — Russ & Daughters

Two blocks away, open since 1914. Russ & Daughters is a smoked fish shop — the counter displays salmon, herring and sturgeon, and customers take their choices seriously. The bagel is hand-rolled, boiled and baked; you notice the difference when you bite into it. The Classic Board: smoked salmon, cream cheese, bagel. Wrapped in paper. Come for breakfast.

⭐ 179 E Houston St · since 1914 · order the Classic Board
🥚 Bacon, egg and cheese — any bodega

Egg, American cheese and bacon cooked on a flat-top griddle, served on a Kaiser roll or bagel, wrapped in foil. The cheese should be fully melted into everything — that's the standard. Restaurants make the same thing but the bodega version has its own character.

⭐ Any bodega · eaten on the street or subway
🍖 Chopped cheese — Harlem and the Bronx

A bodega sandwich specific to Harlem and the Bronx — ground beef, onions and cheese chopped together on a griddle and served on a hoagie roll. Less famous than the pastrami but equally essential to understanding New York. The original is at Hajji's, also known as the Blue Sky Deli, on First Avenue at 110th Street in East Harlem. Simple, fast and exactly what it should be.

⭐ Hajji's, First Ave & 110th St · Harlem
🍕 Pizza by the slice — Joe's Pizza

On Carmine Street in the West Village since 1975 — the reference point. Thin crust, foldable, the right amount of grease. There are tens of thousands of pizza places in the city; Joe's is the standard everything else is measured against. Avoid the lunch rush — go just before or after. Take a slice, fold it, walk.

⭐ 7 Carmine St, West Village · since 1975 · eat standing up
🥗 Dim sum — Flushing, Queens

Manhattan Chinatown is for tourists; Flushing is the real thing. 30 minutes on the 7 train, Main Street: the largest Chinese community outside China. Most regional cuisines never made it to Manhattan. The basement food court of New World Mall alone is worth the trip. Korean supermarkets, Taiwanese desserts, Malaysian curry — all on the same street.

⭐ 7 train to Flushing · better and cheaper than Manhattan Chinatown
🍷 The bodega — how it works

Walk in. The cat will be somewhere. The deli counter is at the back. Order the bacon egg and cheese — specify your bread (roll, bagel, or hero) and your extras (salt pepper ketchup). Pay. Wait two minutes. Leave. If there's a line, there's a reason. If the prices seem high for a bodega, you're in a neighbourhood where the rent is high. Find a different bodega. There are 13,000 of them in New York City.

Off The Beaten Path

Handpicked Hidden Gems in New York

New York's best-kept secrets are mostly hiding in plain sight, ignored because they require leaving the standard tourist loop.

Grand Central Whispering Gallery The acoustic trick in Grand Central that no one uses

In the lower level of Grand Central Terminal, near the entrance to the Oyster Bar restaurant, four tiled arches meet at a corner. Stand at one corner, face the wall, and whisper. Someone standing at the diagonally opposite corner — 15 metres away — will hear you clearly, while no one in between will hear anything. The arches create a focused acoustic path. The effect is immediate and completely real. Free, no sign, no queue. Most of the thousands of people who walk through Grand Central every day have no idea it exists.

The High Line A park 9 metres above street level with the best skyline view in the city

A 2.3-kilometre elevated park built on a disused freight rail line on Manhattan's west side, running from the Meatpacking District to Hudson Yards. Open since 2009, free, and genuinely well-designed — native plantings, art installations, views across the Hudson River. The best section is the stretch between 14th and 20th Streets, where the old rail tracks are preserved in the planting. Go early in the morning before the crowds arrive, or in winter when it's empty.

Governors Island An island 10 minutes from Manhattan that most New Yorkers have never been to

A 172-acre island in New York Harbor, open May to October. The ferry leaves from Pier 6 in lower Manhattan and the Battery Maritime Building in Brooklyn — ticketed; free on Saturdays and Sundays before 11am. Car-free, mostly open green space, with historic fortifications, art installations, and food vendors. The Hills — artificial mounds built from construction debris — offer elevated 360-degree views of the harbour, the Statue of Liberty, and the lower Manhattan skyline. Bike rentals available.

The Met Cloisters — Fort Tryon Park Medieval art in a monastery, hidden in a park at the top of Manhattan

The Metropolitan Museum's medieval art collection, housed in a purpose-built monastery assembled from five actual 12th–15th century French cloisters, transported stone by stone and reconstructed in Fort Tryon Park at the northern tip of Manhattan. The Unicorn Tapestries — seven 15th-century woven panels depicting a unicorn hunt — are here. So are Romanesque chapels, Gothic halls, a medieval herb garden, and views over the Hudson River to the New Jersey Palisades. Admission is covered by the standard Met ticket. Take the A train to 190th Street — about 45 minutes from Midtown. Almost nobody goes.

Greenacre Park — East 51st Street A secret waterfall park hidden between skyscrapers in Midtown

A privately owned, publicly accessible pocket park on East 51st Street between Second and Third Avenues — marked by a small gate that most people walk past without noticing. Inside: a 12-metre waterfall, honey locust trees, moveable chairs, and a snack bar. The waterfall is loud enough to block the sound of Midtown traffic almost completely. A quiet, shaded place to sit in the middle of the loudest part of the city. Free. Open weekdays.

Sylvan Terrace — Washington Heights The neighbourhood that looks like 1900 — and is still lived in

A single block of wooden rowhouses in Washington Heights, built in 1882, that survived the 20th century almost unchanged. Twenty houses, two storeys each, original wooden façades painted in pastels. The street is cobblestone. It sits next to the Morris-Jumel Mansion, the oldest house in Manhattan, in a neighbourhood that most visitors never reach. Take the C train to 163rd Street. The street is free and always open. The Morris-Jumel Mansion next door is ticketed.

✨ Flushing, Queens — the most underrated neighbourhood in New York

Take the 7 train from Times Square to Main Street-Flushing — about 30 minutes. You arrive in the largest Chinese community outside China: a grid of streets dense with restaurants, bakeries, grocery stores, and food courts serving regional Chinese cuisines that don't exist anywhere in Manhattan. The New World Mall basement food court alone contains more interesting food than most of midtown. Also: Korean supermarkets, Taiwanese bubble tea, Malaysian curry. No tourist infrastructure, no English menus everywhere, prices a fraction of Manhattan. This is what New York has always been — a city of arrivals, still arriving.

Scam Prevention

Tourist Traps to Avoid & Smarter Alternatives

New York is a city of speed; its traps work fast too. Know these four and spend your dollars on the real New York.

🦸 TRAP

Costumed characters in Times Square

The photo with Elmo or a superhero starts 'free' and ends with an aggressive tip demand. The square's chain restaurants are the same game in food form.

💡 What to Do Instead: See the square, take your photo, leave in five minutes; eat two avenues over in Hell's Kitchen.
🎭 TRAP

'Discount Broadway tickets' on the street

Hand-sold tickets are fake or void; the tourist always loses.

💡 What to Do Instead: For same-day discounts use the official TKTS booth, or try the shows' own lottery apps.
🗽 TRAP

'Statue of Liberty tour' hawkers

The boats touted around Battery Park don't even get close; imitations of the official ferry are pricey disappointments.

💡 What to Do Instead: Go official with Statue City Cruises, or take the zero-dollar classic: the Staten Island Ferry passes the statue for free.
🚕 TRAP

'Need a taxi?' people at the airport

The unlicensed drivers who approach you at JFK charge triple; the real taxi line is outside with a flat rate.

💡 What to Do Instead: Use the official taxi line (JFK–Manhattan is a flat fare) or ride AirTrain + subway into town: cheapest, and often fastest.
🛡️ The golden rule

In New York, everything that shouts at you is expensive and everything that ignores you is good. The best places don't invite you in — and don't need a queue at the door.
