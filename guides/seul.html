Seoul

Where five-hundred-year-old palaces and neon streets live at once

Overview & Atmosphere

The True Vibe of Seoul

Seoul says speed and grace in the same sentence: a five-hundred-year-old ceremony is reenacted in the courtyard of Gyeongbokgung while, two streets over, a K-pop video breaks records over the world's fastest internet. The curve of hanok roofs and the gleam of glass towers share one frame; at midnight the street stalls still steam and the cafes stay open to dawn. Everything here runs 'ppalli ppalli' (quick, quick) — but the table culture refuses to hurry: time spent around the grill is Seoul's most precious hospitality.

🗣️ Local Concept: Jeong (juhng)

Korean's untranslatable word: the deep attachment and unconditional care woven over time. The stall keeper adding an extra piece to your plate, a host feeding you like their own child — all of it is jeong. Understanding Korea begins with noticing this invisible bond circulating at every table.

3 Unwritten Rules for Seoul

1

Change your map: use Naver or Kakao

Google Maps only half-works in Korea: download Naver Map or Kakao Map for walking and transit routes. Both have English interfaces and show metro exit numbers — in Seoul, a meeting place is given as 'station + exit number'.

2

Learn the table manners: sharing is the rule

The Korean table is built on shared plates: the banchan belong to everyone, and the grill is cooked together at the table. Don't leave your spoon standing in the rice bowl, and pause a beat before the eldest starts — small gestures earn great respect in this city.

3

Tour the palace in hanbok, follow the palace rhythm

Those wearing rented hanbok enter the palaces free: touring Gyeongbokgung in traditional dress is not a tourist gimmick — locals do it too. Time your visit to the changing of the guard — drums in the palace courtyard are the city's most graceful time travel.

Getting Around

Seoul Public Transit & Airport Transfers

The Seoul metro is among the world's best: clean, punctual, English-friendly — with a T-money card the city fits in your palm.

From Incheon, the AREX express reaches Seoul Station in 45 minutes; the all-stop AREX is slower but cheaper. For late-night landings, airport limousine buses fan out by district — taxis are comfortable but costly.

Color-coded lines, numbered exits and English announcements: the metro is Seoul's spine. Buy a T-money card at any convenience store; transfers calculate automatically. Line 2 packs out at rush hour — wait one train, the next comes emptier.

Taxis are reasonable by world standards: orange-and-silver is standard, black 'deluxe' is pricier. The Kakao T app ends the address problem — showing the driver an address in Korean is always the fastest solution.

Seoul is walked neighborhood by neighborhood: Bukchon's slopes, Seongsu's warehouse lanes, Hongdae's young crowds. The Cheonggyecheon stream is a walking corridor through the city's middle — lovelier still under the night lights.

The Owl buses run the main arteries after midnight; city buses reach the gates of Bukhansan and the other mountains. Few places escape the metro — the bus is its scenic complement.

⚡ Pro Tip

Crack the address culture: 'Hongik Univ. Station, Exit 9' is an address. Type the station into Naver Map and follow the exit number — leaving by the wrong exit means an extra ten-minute walk in Seoul.

Off The Beaten Path

Handpicked Hidden Gems in Seoul

Everyone sees Gyeongbokgung; these six are Seoul's hanok-scented, mountain-framed hidden face.

Ikseondong

Cafes, workshops and boutiques in the narrow lanes of restored hanoks: third-wave coffee under traditional roofs. Go on a weekday morning — the lanes fill by afternoon, but the morning is yours.

The Naksan fortress walls

The Naksan stretch of the city walls is lit at night: five-hundred-year-old stones on one side, a million-lighted city on the other. The route from Hyehwa down to Dongdaemun is Seoul's most romantic free activity.

Bukhansan National Park

Granite peaks rising out of the city: metro and bus deliver you to the gate, and within hours you're looking down on the megacity from bare rock. Few cities of millions have such a mountain this close — go on a weekday.

The jjimjilbang experience

Hot pools, salt and ice rooms, naps on heated floors and the sheep-head towel: the jjimjilbang is not a spa but a social institution. You can even stay the night — falling asleep on a warm floor is the Korean way of resetting.

Mangwon Market

A neighborhood market tourists rarely reach: fried-chicken stalls, home-style dishes and friendly prices. Shop, then walk five minutes to the Han River — market plus river picnic is the locals' weekend formula.

Seongsu and Seoul Forest

Old shoe factories became cafes, galleries and pop-up shops; next door, the deer groves of Seoul Forest. Split an afternoon between the two — Seoul's creative present and its green breath side by side.

Local Slang

Useful Everyday Phrases in Seoul

Annyeonghaseyo
(ahn-nyong-ha-SEH-yo)
Hello — with a slight bow; valid at every hour of the day
Gamsahamnida
(kam-sah-ham-NEE-da)
Thank you — from stall to metro, your most-used word
Jal meokgesseumnida
(chal mok-keh-sum-NEE-da)
'I will eat well' — said before the meal; it wins the host's heart
Geonbae!
(gun-BAE)
Cheers! — as the soju rises; turning slightly away from elders while drinking is the custom
Juseyo
(joo-SEH-yo)
Please give me — combined with pointing, the key to the art of menu-free ordering
