Tokyo

The quiet giant of order, neon and perfection

Overview

The Vibe of Tokyo

Tokyo is not one city but a hundred nested ones: five minutes separate the human tide of Shibuya Crossing from the cedar forest of Meiji Shrine. Here the world's densest metro doesn't slip by a second, the smallest ramen shop practices its craft with religious seriousness, and a vending machine serves hot soup. The city is enormous but never rude: everyone flows quietly, every corner is clean, even getting lost is safe. Tokyo's secret is not in its scale but in its detail — the closer you look, the more you see.

💬 Omotenashi
🗣️ /oh-moh-teh-NAH-shee/

The philosophy of Japanese hospitality: service considered before you ask, given without expectation of return. The taxi door that opens by itself, the cashier presenting your package with both hands, the paper bag slipped into plastic when it rains — all omotenashi. There is no tipping, because excellence is already the standard.

Insider Advice

Unwritten Rules for Tokyo

#1
Flow quietly: no phone calls on the train, no eating while walking Tokyo's order runs on invisible rules: no phone conversations on the metro, food eaten standing rather than strolling, and your trash travels in your bag — bins are rare. Compliance is the tourist's invisible visa: follow the rules and the city opens its doors.
#2
Carry both an IC card and cash A Suica or Pasmo card (loadable onto your phone) works everywhere from metro to vending machine; but small ramen shops and temples still want cash. In ticket-machine restaurants, buy your slip from the machine first — the table is set without money changing hands.
#3
Tour by neighborhood; don't try to finish the city Tokyo cannot be 'seen' in one pass: Asakusa's temple morning, Shibuya's neon night and Yanaka's old lanes are separate cities. Pick two or three neighborhoods a day and connect them by train — fewer districts, more depth: that is the Tokyo formula.
Off the Beaten Path

Hidden Gems in Tokyo

💎 HIDDEN GEM
Yanaka Ginza
The market street of a shitamachi quarter that survived war and earthquake: cat figurines, craft shops, and the 'sunset stairs' in evening light. With its temple courtyards and cedar-shaded old cemetery — Tokyo's slowest afternoon.
💎 HIDDEN GEM
Shimokitazawa
Second-hand record shops, vintage stores, tiny stages and coffee roasters: Tokyo's bohemian village. Two stops from the main lines, years away in atmosphere — Sunday afternoon is its liveliest hour.
💎 HIDDEN GEM
Todoroki Valley
A stream valley shaded by bamboo and maple in the middle of Setagaya: a footpath, a red bridge and a temple tea house at the valley's end. The metropolis' sound disappears in thirty seconds — Tokyo's most surprising nature escape.
💎 HIDDEN GEM
Kagurazaka
The stone lanes of an old geisha district now filled with French bistros and kappo restaurants: Tokyo's 'little Paris'. The red-lantern staircases in its side alleys are the evening walk's reward.
💎 HIDDEN GEM
The Nezu Museum garden
An Asian art collection beside Omotesando, with a hidden Japanese garden behind: tea houses, mossy stones, a lotus pond. From fashion's heart to stillness in two minutes — the elegant summary of Tokyo's contrasts.
💎 HIDDEN GEM
Omoide Yokocho
A narrow postwar izakaya alley at the foot of Shinjuku Station: smoke, lantern light and shoulder-to-shoulder counters. It earns its name, 'Memory Lane' — a yakitori skewer and a beer are the ticket for time travel.
Food & Flavors

What to Taste

Tokyo holds more Michelin stars than any city on earth, but its real miracle is how extraordinary the ordinary places are: even the station ramen man is a craftsman.

🍴 MUST TASTE
Sushi: from counter to omakase
From Toyosu market's morning sushi to the neighborhood master's counter: fresh fish is the baseline here; craft is the difference. Standing sushi bars (tachigui) are excellent and affordable — eating nigiri with your hand is legitimate, and dipping fish-side into the soy is the etiquette.
🍴 MUST TASTE
The ramen ritual
Buy a slip from the ticket machine, take a counter seat, wait for your bowl: from tonkotsu to shoyu, each shop masters one soup. Slurping isn't rude, it's a compliment — and a queue means you're at the right door.
🍴 MUST TASTE
Izakaya night
At the Japanese tavern the small plates keep coming: yakitori skewers, edamame, karaage fried chicken. Start with 'toriaezu nama' (a beer, to begin) — the izakaya is where Tokyo exhales after work.
🍴 MUST TASTE
Konbini breakfast
7-Eleven, Lawson, FamilyMart: the Japanese convenience store is a kitchen — onigiri, the famous egg sandwich (tamago sando) and hot coffee. Cheap, fresh and astonishingly good: whoever scorns the konbini misses Tokyo's first lesson.
🍴 MUST TASTE
Depachika discovery
The basement food halls of the luxury department stores: bento boxes, mochi sweets, a hundred kinds of pickles. Discount stickers appear near closing time — a depachika bento eaten in a park is Tokyo's elegant picnic trick.
🍴 MUST TASTE
Wagashi and a matcha pause
Wagashi, the delicate sweets shaped by the season, beside a deep bowl of matcha: at a temple tea house or an old confectioner's. Sweet first, tea after — bitterness and sweetness speak in turn.
Watch Out

Tourist Traps to Avoid

❌
In the nightlife district, touts offering 'free drinks, great bar' (especially the English-speaking ones) steer you to venues with inflated bills; objecting is hard.
✅ Better Alternative: Never follow a street invitation to a bar — keep the rule absolute. For a drinking night choose Golden Gai's signposted bars or izakaya chains; ask about the cover charge at the door, and the answer will be clear.
❌
In some tourist zones, windows shouting 'fresh fish' sell mediocre sushi at master prices; an English-menu crowd can mislead.
✅ Better Alternative: Choose the queued tachigui bars, the market-hall counters or the lunchtime set menus of sushi masters. Clear price list, full counter, a chef chatting with Japanese customers — that's the right place.
❌
The JR Pass price has risen: a traveler staying in Tokyo with one or two day trips never earns the pass back.
✅ Better Alternative: Calculate your route: for long itineraries like a Kyoto–Osaka round trip the pass can still make sense, but for a Tokyo-focused trip Suica plus single tickets nearly always wins. Five minutes of math, serious savings.
❌
Tokyo looks modern, but small restaurants, temples and some bars run cash-only; the card-only tourist stalls at the door.
✅ Better Alternative: Always carry 10,000–20,000 yen in cash; the konbini ATMs (7-Eleven) take foreign cards without fuss. Coins help at ticket-machine shops too — cash is still a key in Tokyo.
