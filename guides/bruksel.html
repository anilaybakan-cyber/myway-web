Brussels

Art nouveau, comic-strip walls and Europe's untidy but lovable capital

✍️ MyWay Editorial Team • 🛡️ Verified Local Guide
Overview & Atmosphere

The True Vibe of Brussels

Brussels doesn't try to charm you at first sight: art nouveau mansions stand beside glass EU towers, and the clatter of a flea market is two streets from the elegant arcades. But your breath catches the first time you step into the Grand Place, you come face to face with a Tintin mural on a wall, and in a frietkot queue you hear three languages at once. This city isn't polished, it's layered — it gets more beautiful the more you peel. Take your time; Brussels wins you over on day two.

🗣️ Local Concept: Zwanze (zvahn-zeh)

Brussels' own brand of humour: self-mocking, gently irreverent towards authority, sweetly sarcastic. Making a statue of a peeing boy your national symbol — that is Zwanze.

3 Unwritten Rules for Brussels

1

Stay flexible in a bilingual city

Brussels is officially bilingual: street signs come in French and Dutch, and one square can have two names. French dominates, but English works everywhere; start with 'bonjour' and the rest follows.

2

The beauty is up high and in the courtyards

Brussels' façades can look tired, but the carvings above doorways, the art nouveau balconies and the inner courtyards will surprise you. Walk without looking up and peeking through gates, and you miss half the city.

3

Give Sunday morning to the flea market

The Jeu de Balle flea market has been set up every morning for 150 years; Sunday is its fullest day. Go early, haggle, then have breakfast in the Marolles cafés — this is Brussels' most local morning.

Getting Around

Brussels Public Transit & Airport Transfers

The centre is walkable, the hills are tiring, the metro-tram network is a friend: combine them and forget the idea of a car.

✈️

Airport to the centre

The train from Zaventem reaches Central in 20 minutes. Read the small print on any ticket that says 'Brussels South Charleroi': that airport is an hour from the city and needs a bus transfer.

🚇

The metro and tram network

The STIB/MIVB network wraps the city; tap your contactless bank card at the gate and the daily fare cap is calculated automatically. Look at the art in the metro stations — some are small galleries.

🚶

The centre on foot — but hilly

Most places are walkable from the Grand Place, but the city is hillier than it looks — the Sablon and the Palace sit on the ridge. Use the shortcuts like the lift by the Congress column down to the Lower Town.

🚄

Day-trip trains

Belgian rail is a day-tripper's paradise: Bruges and Ghent in an hour, Antwerp in 45 minutes. Weekend tickets are discounted; make Brussels your base and sweep Flanders.

🚲

Villo! and the bike lanes

The shared bikes are cheap, but with the hills and cobbles, cycling Brussels is for the experienced: the canalside and the parks are lovely, the centre's traffic takes courage.

💡 Metro tip

The metro art tour is free: a giant Tintin wall at Stockel, surrealist works at Porte de Hal. You've already bought the ticket — hop off at a few stations, look around, catch the next train.

Neighborhood Guide

Where to Stay in Brussels: Best Areas

Brussels is a different city in every quarter: the medieval centre, elegant Sablon, bohemian Saint-Gilles, the bureaucrats' European Quarter.

Grand Place and the Ilot Sacré

Most central

Waking up two streets from the world's most beautiful square: museums, arcades and the smell of waffles at your door. Lively at night, touristy on the surface — the practical classic for a first visit.

📍 Grand Place two steps away • Walk to everything • Lively at night

Sablon

Most elegant

The hilltop of antique dealers, master chocolatiers and galleries: a weekend antiques market, and the city's smartest cafés around it. Walking distance to the Royal Museums; prices match the elegance.

📍 Antique dealers • Master chocolatiers • Next to the museums

Saint-Gilles

Most bohemian

The district of art nouveau façades, market squares and young coffee roasters: the Horta Museum is here. Few tourists, real neighbourhood life; ten minutes to the centre by tram.

📍 Art nouveau streets • Neighbourhood market • Young cafés

Sainte-Catherine and Dansaert

Most delicious

The old fish market square and the Flemish designers' street: the city's best restaurants and bars cluster here. Five minutes to the centre, long dinners guaranteed.

📍 Restaurant density • Design shops • Five minutes to centre

The European Quarter (Quartier Européen)

Practical pick

The EU institutions' district: suit traffic on weekdays, calm and discounted hotels at weekends. Next to Cinquantenaire park; sensible if your business is with the EU or you're a weekend bargain hunter.

📍 Cheap at weekends • Cinquantenaire park • Metro to centre 10 min
Seasons & Weather

Best Time to Visit Brussels

Brussels' calendar is full of surprises: a flower carpet, a medieval pageant, a light festival — whatever the sky decides.

Spring

Apr – Jun
Greenhouse Season

The parks turn green, the terraces go up, and the year's most special rendezvous arrives: the Royal Greenhouses of Laeken open to the public for just a few weeks. The weather is capricious, but the city is at its freshest.

Summer

Jul – Aug
Festivals on the Square

In July the Grand Place travels back in time with the Ommegang medieval pageant; in mid-August (in even years) the square is covered by a giant carpet of begonias. The city has the calm of holiday season, and festivals come one after another.

Autumn

Sep – Nov
Museum and Beer Season

In September the beer festival fills the Grand Place with wooden casks and the museums begin their evening events; the leaves paint the Cinquantenaire gold. The crowds thin, and the estaminets grow warm.

Winter

Dec – Feb
Light and Punsch

In December Winter Wonders takes over the city: the Christmas market on the fish market square, a giant Ferris wheel by the canal, a sound-and-light show on the Grand Place. January and February are museum-and-estaminet season: drizzle outside, a quadrupel inside.

Culinary Culture

Authentic Local Dining & Food Secrets in Brussels

The Brussels table is generous: mussel pots, double-fried frites, chocolate in century-old arcades and the deepest beer menu in the world.

🦪

Moules-frites — the national pot

A pot of mussels with white wine and celery, frites on the side: Belgium's national dish. In season from September to April; using an empty shell as tongs is the proper technique.

⭐ Around Sainte-Catherine · in season
🍟

The frietkot ritual

A cone of double-fried frites with andalouse sauce on top: in Brussels the frietkots work late into the night and every neighbourhood has its favourite. If the queue is long, you're in the right place.

⭐ In a cone · from the stand with the queue
🧇

The gaufre — plain school

The Brussels waffle is light and rectangular, the Liège dense and caramelised. Tourist shops sell them under mountains of strawberries and chocolate; locals eat theirs hot and plain, icing sugar at most.

⭐ Hot and plain · no to the fruit mountain
🍫

A praline arcade crawl

In the covered Galeries Royales lives the haute couture of chocolate: you're in the city where the praline was invented. Have your box picked piece by piece, and don't forget the sea-salt caramel.

⭐ In the Galeries Royales · pick the box yourself
🥘

Stoemp and carbonnade

Stoemp: mashed potato with vegetables, topped with sausage or a fried egg — Brussels home cooking. Carbonnade flamande is beef braised in beer. Eat both in an estaminet, at a wooden table.

⭐ In an estaminet · home-cooking mode
🍺

A lambic and gueuze tasting

Spontaneously fermented lambic, blended champagne-style, becomes gueuze: sour, alive, unforgettable. The historic Cantillon brewery by the canal still works with 1900s methods — the tour includes a tasting.

⭐ At Cantillon · be open to sour
🍷 The beer rule

Don't panic if the beer menu is as thick as a book: tell the waiter sweet, sour or dark, and they'll solve the rest. Every beer arrives in its own glass — photographing the glass isn't rude, it's tradition.

Off The Beaten Path

Handpicked Hidden Gems in Brussels

Everyone sees the Grand Place; these six open up Brussels' layers.

✨ The house where art nouveau was born

The Horta Museum

Victor Horta's own house in Saint-Gilles: the staircase is a spiral of light, every door handle a design lesson. In the home of the man who invented art nouveau, you see the movement at its purest. Tickets are limited — book ahead.

✨ A 150-year-old flea market

The Jeu de Balle Flea Market

The market set up every morning in the heart of the Marolles: old records, dog-eared Tintins, silver forks, strange keepsakes. Haggling included, breakfast in the surrounding cafés — Brussels' most honest morning.

✨ The ninth art on the walls

The Comic Book Route

More than sixty giant murals hide on the city's walls, from Tintin to Lucky Luke: take the map and hunt street by street. Start with the Tintin wall in the centre and collect your way towards the Marolles.

✨ The city's best free terrace

The MIM rooftop

The rooftop café of the Musical Instruments Museum, in the art nouveau Old England building, looks out over the Lower Town: domes, rooftops, the Atomium in the distance. The museum itself is a surprise too — you tour it listening to the instruments through headphones.

✨ A winter garden of iron and glass

The Royal Greenhouses of Laeken

King Leopold II's art nouveau glass palaces: camellias, palms, domed winter gardens. Open only a few weeks a year (April–May) — if your visit coincides, cancel every plan and come here.

✨ On top of the triumphal arch

The Cinquantenaire arch

You reach the terrace of the park's colossal triumphal arch through the army museum: all of Brussels at your feet, from the EU towers to the old town. The park is for picnics, the museums are the rain plan.

✨ Surrealism tip

Book the Magritte Museum for the afternoon: the morning crowds belong to school groups. On the way out, watch the sunset from the Mont des Arts garden two steps away — clouds in bowler hats are not guaranteed, but they happen.

Scam Prevention

Tourist Traps to Avoid & Smarter Alternatives

Brussels' traps are concentrated in two streets; know them, and the rest of the city is honest.

🦞 TRAP

The insistent waiters of Rue des Bouchers

The iced seafood displays and arm-pulling waiters of the restaurant street are the classic trap: lukewarm mussels, a swollen bill, and the 'free' starter invoiced at the end.

💡 What to Do Instead: Take a parallel street or walk to Place Sainte-Catherine: the same mussel pot, half the price, local customers.
🧇 TRAP

A strawberry mountain on top, a stale waffle below

The neon shopfronts around Manneken Pis bury the waffle under fruit, cream and sugar: the sweetest way to hide stale batter.

💡 What to Do Instead: Order it hot and plain: if the batter is fresh, it needs nothing. A Liège waffle straight off a street cart's iron beats most shops.
🍫 TRAP

'Belgian chocolate' outlets

The 'factory price' signs along the tourist drag sell industrial chocolate in gift boxes at triple the price.

💡 What to Do Instead: Climb to the Sablon: in the masters' shops the pralines are picked piece by piece; the price difference is smaller than you think, the difference on the tongue is huge.
⚛️ TRAP

Going to the Atomium with the wrong expectations

The Atomium is mesmerising from outside; inside it's a long queue plus a modest exhibition, and the disappointment comes bundled with the ticket.

💡 What to Do Instead: Settle for photos from outside and smile at Mini-Europe next door; if you do go in, go for the evening lights, not the exhibits.
🛡️ Golden rule

In Brussels, avoid anywhere that tries to pull you in from the doorway: a good estaminet has locals sitting inside, not a waiter standing outside.

Local Slang

Useful Everyday Phrases in Brussels

Brussels is bilingual; these five French phrases open every door, and a 'dank u wel' earns you a Flemish smile as a bonus.

🗣️ Bonjour
Good day — the mandatory first word of every exchange; skip it and you start cold
🗣️ Merci
Thank you — keep 'dank u wel' in reserve for the Flemish side
🗣️ S'il vous plaît
Please — in Belgium the waiter also says it when setting down your plate
🗣️ Santé!
Cheers! — no glass is raised without eye contact
🗣️ Une gueuze, s'il vous plaît
'A gueuze, please' — this sentence promotes you from tourist to guest
