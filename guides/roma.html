Rome

Three thousand years of history — and a city that still belongs to the living

✍️ MyWay Editorial Team • 🛡️ Verified Local Guide
Overview & Atmosphere

The True Vibe of Rome

Rome is the most layered city on earth. A Republican temple sits beneath a medieval church beneath a Baroque piazza. Ancient aqueducts run beneath traffic. The Forum was a cattle market before it was an archaeological site. Most visitors spend their time moving between the headline monuments — the Colosseum, the Vatican, the Trevi Fountain — and miss the city that lives inside and between them. The real Rome is not a museum. It is a functioning, chaotic, magnificent city of three million people who eat lunch at 13:30, drink espresso standing up, and treat the ruins around them as background. The question is not whether to see the Colosseum. It is what you do when you look up from the map.

🗣️ Local Concept: Aperitivo (ah-peh-REE-tee-voh)

The early evening ritual before dinner — a drink (Aperol Spritz, Campari, Negroni) taken between roughly 18:00 and 20:00 in a bar or piazza. Not a meal. Not a party. A decompression of the day. In Rome this happens outdoors wherever possible — on steps, on piazza walls, at small tables spilling onto cobblestones. It is the best single hour of the Roman day and it costs the price of a drink.

3 Unwritten Rules for Rome

1

Never eat on the main tourist street

Every tourist neighbourhood in Rome has a main street lined with restaurants displaying photographs of pasta and waiters hailing you from the door. Walk past all of them. Turn down a side street, find something with no English menu and no photograph of the food, and eat there. The price will be lower and the food will be better. This is one of the most reliable rules in Rome.

2

Espresso at the bar, always standing

An espresso in Rome is drunk standing at the bar counter, not sitting at a table. Sitting doubles or triples the price — you are paying for the table, not the coffee. A standing espresso at the bar costs around €1–1.50. Ask for un caffè. Drink it in two sips. Leave. This is how Rome operates before 10am.

3

Book the big things in advance

The Vatican Museums, the Borghese Gallery, and the Colosseum all require advance booking — some weeks or months ahead in peak season. Showing up without a reservation at the Vatican Museums in July means hours in a queue. The Borghese Gallery allows only two-hour timed slots and turns away walk-ins. Book before you arrive, not the night before.

Getting Around

Rome Public Transit & Airport Transfers

Rome's historic centre is compact and best navigated on foot. Public transport fills the gaps. The metro is limited but useful; buses and trams cover everything else.

The Leonardo Express train runs directly from Fiumicino to Roma Termini every 30 minutes, taking 32 minutes. It is the most reliable option. Regional trains (FL1) are slower and cheaper but stop at multiple stations. Taxis from Fiumicino to the city centre are fixed rate — agree it before you get in. Private transfer apps (Uber Black operates in Rome) are reliable but more expensive than the train.

Rome has only two metro lines: A (orange) and B (blue), intersecting at Termini. Line A reaches the Vatican (Ottaviano), Trastevere area (via bus from Spagna), and the Spanish Steps. Line B reaches the Colosseum (Colosseo stop). The historic centre — Pantheon, Piazza Navona, Campo de' Fiori, Trastevere — is not served by metro. Single tickets cost €1.50 and are valid for 100 minutes across all transport. Validate before boarding.

Rome's bus network is extensive but slow in traffic. Bus 40 and 64 connect Termini to the Vatican. Tram 8 runs from Largo Argentina to Trastevere — the most useful tram in the historic centre. Bus 23 follows the Tiber from the Vatican south to Testaccio. The same €1.50 ticket works on all services. Download the Moovit or Google Maps app for real-time routes — the official timetables are aspirational.

The Pantheon to the Colosseum is 25 minutes on foot through the Forum. Campo de' Fiori to Trastevere is 10 minutes across the bridge. Piazza Navona to the Spanish Steps is 20 minutes. Rome's historic centre is built for walking. The streets that matter — the passages, the courtyards, the early Christian churches hidden behind unassuming doors — are all between the monuments, not at them.

Rome's official taxis are white and metered. Take them from the official taxi ranks (not from anyone approaching you). From Termini to the historic centre costs around €12–18. Fixed fares apply to and from the airports. Uber Black operates in Rome but is pricier than official taxis. Never accept rides from unmarked cars or drivers approaching in airports or train stations.

⚡ Pro Tip

Tram 8 runs from Largo Argentina — the site of Caesar's assassination — through the Jewish Ghetto, across the Tiber, and into Trastevere, ending at Casaletto. For €1.50, it is the most useful single journey in Rome: from the ancient centre to the most atmospheric neighbourhood in the city, in under 15 minutes. Take it in the early evening when the light is at its best on the river.

Off The Beaten Path

Handpicked Hidden Gems in Rome

Rome hides its most extraordinary things in plain sight — behind unassuming doors, beneath ordinary churches, at the end of streets every tourist passes without looking up.

Aventine Keyhole (Knights of Malta Priory)

On the Aventine Hill, in the gate of the Priory of the Knights of Malta, is a keyhole. Peer through it and the perfectly framed dome of St. Peter's Basilica appears at the end of a manicured garden path — three countries visible at once: Italy, the garden of the Sovereign Order of Malta, and the Vatican. Designed in the 18th century by architect and engraver Giovanni Battista Piranesi. The alignment may or may not be intentional — no one has ever been entirely certain. Free entry, accessible at all hours. Queue of one at a time.

Basilica di San Clemente — near the Colosseum

Three minutes from the Colosseum, an unremarkable-looking church hides one of the most extraordinary layered sites in Rome. The ground-floor 12th-century basilica is beautiful and free to enter. Below it is a 4th-century early Christian basilica with medieval frescoes. Below that, a 2nd-century Roman house and a functioning Mithraic temple — you can still hear an ancient stream running underground. The experience of descending through twenty centuries in one building is unlike anything else in Rome. Upper church free. Underground excavations ticketed (€10), advance booking required online.

Palazzo Doria Pamphilj — Via del Corso

On the Via del Corso, the most central street in Rome, a door leads into the largest privately-owned palace in the city — still occupied by the Doria Pamphilj family, whose ancestor commissioned Velázquez's Portrait of Pope Innocent X. The gallery holds Caravaggio, Raphael, Titian, and Velázquez. The audioguide is narrated by Prince Jonathan Doria Pamphilj himself. Unlike the Vatican, there are no crowds. Unlike the Borghese, it is almost unknown to non-Italian visitors. Ticketed entry (€16), advance booking required.

Quartiere Coppedè — Trieste neighbourhood

Between 1913 and 1926, architect Gino Coppedè built a neighbourhood of 26 small palaces and 17 villas in the Trieste district — a collision of Art Nouveau, Baroque, Gothic, and Greek revival so extreme it looks like a film set. Swagged stone garlands, bronze spiders, fairy-tale arches, elaborate ironwork. An entire tram stop away from any tourist route. Almost no one goes. Completely free to walk through. Enter through the arch on Via Tagliamento.

Giardino degli Aranci — Aventine Hill

The Garden of Oranges on the Aventine Hill is a formal garden of bitter orange trees, pine umbrella trees, and rose bushes, with a terrace at one end that offers one of the finest panoramic views of Rome — the dome of St. Peter's above the city's terracotta rooftops and the Tiber below. Almost entirely unknown to non-Italian visitors. Two minutes from the Aventine Keyhole. Free entry. Open from early morning to sunset.

Largo di Torre Argentina — Historic Centre

In the middle of central Rome, surrounded by traffic, a sunken square reveals four Republican-era temples from the 3rd and 2nd centuries BC — among the oldest ruins visible in the city. This is the site where Julius Caesar was assassinated on 15 March 44 BC. A cat sanctuary, run by volunteers, houses over a hundred cats among the ruins. The cats have been there for generations. The archaeological site is ticketed. The cats can be seen for free from the street level.
