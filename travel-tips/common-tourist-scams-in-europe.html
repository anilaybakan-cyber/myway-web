The Most Common Tourist Scams in Europe, and How to Avoid Each One

Friendship bracelets, petition clipboards, the gold ring trick, fake police, rigged taxi meters and the ATM currency trap: how each scam works and the one-line response that stops it.

✍️ MyWay Editorial Team 📅 Published 15 October 2026 🔄 Updated 15 October 2026 ⏱️ 10 min read

Europe's cities are generally safe for visitors, and violent crime against tourists is rare. What you are much more likely to encounter are small, well-rehearsed tricks designed to separate you from a few euros, your attention or your phone. They work because they rely on politeness and surprise. Knowing the script in advance removes both. Below are the scams we hear about most often, how they work, and how to respond.

Distraction and pickpocketing

The friendship bracelet

Where: around Sacré-Cœur in Paris, the Spanish Steps in Rome and other busy viewpoints.
How it works: a friendly person asks you to hold out a finger "for a quick demonstration", ties a string bracelet onto your wrist, then demands payment, sometimes with friends standing close.
What to do: keep your hands in your pockets, do not stop, and say a firm "no" while walking on. Never offer your hand.

The petition clipboard

Where: major squares and metro exits in many capitals.
How it works: someone, often presenting themselves as deaf or collecting for a charity, asks you to sign a petition. The clipboard covers your bag or phone while an accomplice picks your pocket, or you are pressured into a "donation" after signing.
What to do: do not sign anything on the street. Keep walking.

The spill or the dropped item

How it works: something is "accidentally" spilled on you (ketchup, coffee, bird droppings), and a helpful stranger rushes in to clean it, or someone drops coins in front of you. In the confusion, a wallet or phone disappears.
What to do: decline help, step away, secure your bag first and clean up later somewhere quiet.

Crowded transport

Busy metro lines and tram routes that serve major sights are classic pickpocketing environments, particularly around the doors as they close. Wear your backpack on your front in crowds, keep your phone in a zipped or front pocket, and be alert when a group suddenly pushes around you at the doors.

Money tricks

The gold ring

Where: Paris riverbanks and bridges, among others.
How it works: someone "finds" a gold ring on the ground next to you, asks if it is yours, then offers to let you keep it for a small sum. The ring is brass.
What to do: "Not mine," and walk on.

The ATM currency trap (dynamic currency conversion)

Where: everywhere, especially standalone cash machines in tourist areas.
How it works: the machine or card terminal offers to charge you "in your home currency". It sounds helpful but usually applies a poor exchange rate, sometimes several percent worse than your bank's. Some independent ATMs also charge high withdrawal fees.
What to do: always choose to be charged in the local currency, and prefer ATMs attached to real bank branches. We explain this in detail in our guide to paying abroad.

The change trick

How it works: you pay with a large note; the seller is slow, distracted, or claims you gave a smaller note.
What to do: say the value of the note out loud as you hand it over, and count your change before leaving.

Transport scams

The taxi with "no meter"

How it works: the driver says the meter is broken and quotes a high flat price, or takes a long route. At airports, unofficial drivers approach you inside the arrivals hall.
What to do: use the official taxi rank or a ride-hailing app; agree on the fare or insist on the meter before you get in. Many airports (such as Rome's Fiumicino to the city centre) have official fixed fares for taxis; look them up in advance.

Fake ticket helpers

How it works: at metro or train ticket machines, a "helper" offers to assist, then either pockets your change, sells you the wrong ticket, or watches your PIN.
What to do: politely refuse help; if you are stuck, go to the staffed ticket office.

Social scams

The friendly bar invitation

Where: reported in several nightlife districts, including around Istanbul's Taksim and Beyoğlu.
How it works: a friendly stranger strikes up a conversation and suggests a bar "he knows". The bill is enormous and staff become intimidating when you object.
What to do: choose your own bars, check prices before ordering, and be cautious with spontaneous invitations from people you just met on the street.

The dropped shoe-shine brush

Where: Istanbul's historic peninsula.
How it works: a shoe-shiner drops his brush in front of you. You pick it up, he thanks you warmly and offers a "free" shine as thanks, then asks for an inflated price.
What to do: it is fine to be kind and hand the brush back, but decline the shine.

Fake police

How it works: people claiming to be plain-clothes police ask to check your wallet "for counterfeit money" or drugs, and remove cash.
What to do: real police will rarely ask to inspect your cash on the street. Ask to see identification and offer to walk to the nearest police station or call the emergency number (112 works across the EU and in Turkey).

Five habits that prevent almost everything

  1. Keep walking. Most scams need you to stop. A polite "no, thank you" without breaking stride ends the conversation.
  2. Hands in pockets in hotspots. It protects your wallet and stops bracelet tricks.
  3. Phone off the café table. Phones left on outdoor tables are among the easiest items to snatch.
  4. Carry only what you need. One card and a little cash in your day bag; a backup card in the hotel safe.
  5. Local currency, official taxis, staffed ticket offices.

If something does happen

Call your bank immediately to block cards (save the number somewhere other than your phone), use your phone's "find my device" feature to lock it, and file a police report for insurance; many insurers will not reimburse without one. Your embassy or consulate can help with an emergency travel document if your passport is stolen.
