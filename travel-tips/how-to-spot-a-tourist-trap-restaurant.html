12 Signs a Restaurant Is a Tourist Trap (and How to Find Where Locals Eat)

Laminated photo menus, touts at the door, menus in eight languages: the reliable warning signs of tourist-trap restaurants in Europe, and practical ways to find better food nearby.

✍️ MyWay Editorial Team 📅 Published 15 October 2026 🔄 Updated 15 October 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read

Eating badly and overpaying for it is one of the most common travel regrets, and it is almost always avoidable. Tourist-trap restaurants are not hidden; they share a set of very recognisable habits. Once you know them, you will spot them from across the square. Here are the twelve signs we use when we review restaurants for our guides, followed by the techniques that reliably lead to better meals.

The warning signs

1. Someone outside is trying to bring you in

A host on the pavement waving a menu at passers-by is the clearest signal of all. Restaurants that are good enough to fill with regulars do not need to recruit customers on the street. In many historic centres this practice is so strongly associated with poor value that some cities have tried to restrict it.

2. The menu has large photos of every dish

Laminated menus with faded photos of pizza, paella and schnitzel side by side are designed for people who do not know the local cuisine and cannot read the language. Local restaurants describe dishes in words, often on a short, handwritten or daily-printed menu.

3. The menu is translated into five or more languages

A menu in Italian and English is normal. A menu with flags for eight languages tells you who the restaurant is cooking for.

4. The menu is enormous

A kitchen that offers pizza, pasta, burgers, sushi, curry and a "typical local plate" cannot make all of them fresh. Short menus usually mean fresh ingredients and dishes the kitchen is genuinely good at.

5. Dishes that the city is not known for, or that do not exist locally

"Spaghetti bolognese" in Bologna (locals serve ragù with tagliatelle), "paella" for dinner on Barcelona's main boulevard (paella is traditionally a Valencian lunch dish) or fettuccine Alfredo in Rome are classic tells. If the menu caters to a foreign idea of the cuisine, the cooking usually follows.

6. It sits directly on the most famous square or view

You are paying for the location. Sometimes that is worth it for a drink at sunset, but for a full meal, walking just two or three streets away almost always improves both the food and the bill.

7. Prices are missing, or listed "per 100 g"

Fish and seafood priced by weight are legitimate in many coastal places, but in tourist areas it is a common way to produce a shocking bill. Always ask for the weight and total price before the fish is cooked.

8. Food that arrives too quickly

Risotto in four minutes is not risotto. Very fast service for dishes that need time usually means pre-cooked, reheated food.

9. It is full at 18:00 and empty at 21:30 (in Spain or Italy)

In southern Europe locals eat dinner late: often after 20:30 in Italy and after 21:00 in Spain. A restaurant busy at 18:00 in Madrid is busy with visitors. Conversely, a place that is packed late in the evening with families speaking the local language is a very good sign.

10. Unexpected charges on the bill

Some charges are legitimate and local, such as the coperto (cover charge) in Italy, which should be printed on the menu. Be wary of unexplained items: bread you did not order, "service" added on top of a cover charge, or a different price than the one on the menu.

11. Glowing reviews that all sound the same

Many short, generic five-star reviews posted in a short period, especially in a language other than the local one, are worth treating with suspicion. Read the most recent three-star reviews; they are usually the most honest.

12. The view is the only thing anyone mentions

If every review praises the view and nobody names a dish, you have your answer.

How to find better food, fast

  • Walk five to ten minutes away from the main sight. This simple move fixes most problems.
  • Follow office workers at lunchtime. Places with set lunch menus (menu del día in Spain, menu du jour in France, menu del giorno in Italy, Mittagstisch in Germany) are designed for regulars and are often excellent value.
  • Look for a short menu that changes. A chalkboard or a printed daily sheet is a good sign.
  • Eat at local times. You will get the full menu, the freshest food and the real atmosphere.
  • Use markets. Covered markets such as La Boqueria's quieter back stalls in Barcelona, Mercato Centrale in Florence or Kadıköy market in Istanbul offer food stalls and counters where locals eat.
  • Search in the local language. Searching for "trattoria" or "osteria" plus a neighbourhood name, or reading reviews written in the local language, filters out much of the tourist noise.
  • Ask a specific question. "Where do you eat on your day off?" gets a better answer from a hotel receptionist or a shopkeeper than "where is a good restaurant?".

When a "trap" is fine

Not every touristy place is a rip-off. A drink on a famous square, paying a premium for a view at sunset, or a classic café with history (think of the grand cafés of Vienna) can be worth the price if you know what you are paying for. The problem is not paying for an experience; it is paying a premium for something that is worse than what is available around the corner.
